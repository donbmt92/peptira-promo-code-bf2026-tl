## A Course Is a Production Schedule

A research lab buys materials for experiments whose timing and outcome are uncertain. A teaching lab is different. A laboratory course is closer to a production schedule: a known number of sections, meeting on known days, running known exercises, each consuming a predictable amount of material per student group. The uncertainty is not in the science but in the headcount and in the margin for error that students inevitably need.

## Building the Demand Model

The heart of teaching-lab purchasing is a demand model: a calculation of how much of each material the course needs, built up from sections, groups and exercises. It does not need to be complicated. A spreadsheet with a row per exercise and a few columns is enough.

### The inputs

For each exercise in the course that uses a given material, you need:

1. **Number of sections** running the exercise.
2. **Groups per section**, based on expected enrollment and group size.
3. **Runs per group**, how many times each group performs the procedure in that exercise.
4. **Amount per run**, the quantity of material each run consumes.
5. **Waste factor**, an allowance for spills, errors, repeats and material left in tubes.
6. **Preparation and demonstration**, material used by instructors or TAs to prepare stock solutions, run demonstrations or practice the exercise beforehand.
7. **Makeup and contingency**, material for makeup sessions or a section that needs to repeat an exercise.

### The calculation

For each exercise:

Base use = sections × groups per section × runs per group × amount per run

Total for exercise = base use × (1 + waste factor) + preparation + makeup

Sum across all exercises that use the material, and you have the course total.

### Choosing a waste factor

Student work generates more waste than research work: first attempts, mis-pipetting, dropped tubes, repeated runs when something goes wrong. A waste factor of 15% to 30% is a common starting point for introductory courses, lower for advanced courses with experienced students. The best source is your own past records: compare how much was bought last year with the base use you can calculate, and the difference is your historical waste.

If you do not have records, start with a conservative estimate and record actual usage this spring. Next year's model will be better.

### A worked example

Suppose a fictional spring course has four lab sections. Expected enrollment is 24 students per section, working in pairs, giving 12 groups per section. Two exercises use a particular material.

| Exercise | Sections | Groups/section | Runs/group | Amount/run | Base use | Waste 25% | Prep/demo | Makeup | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Week 4 exercise | 4 | 12 | 2 | 0.5 units | 48 units | 12 units | 4 units | 3 units | 67 units |
| Week 9 exercise | 4 | 12 | 3 | 0.25 units | 36 units | 9 units | 3 units | 2 units | 50 units |
| **Course total** | | | | | | | | | **117 units** |

"Units" here stands for whatever measure the material is sold and used in. The numbers are invented to show the method.

If the material is sold in sizes of 25 units and 100 units, the course needs at least 117 units, which could be one 100-unit size plus one 25-unit size, giving 125. The 8-unit surplus is a reasonable margin. Whether to buy one large and one small, or five small, depends on prices, storage and how the material is handled once opened.

### Preparation is often underestimated

New coordinators often forget the material TAs use to practice exercises before the first section, to prepare stock solutions, and to troubleshoot when something does not work. For a new exercise, preparation can be a substantial share of total use. Ask the TAs who ran the course last time how much they actually used.

### Repeat the model for every material

Do the same calculation for each material the course uses. Some will appear in one exercise; others in many. The result is a list of course totals, which becomes the basis for the order.

## From Demand to an Order List

### Subtract what you already have

Check the teaching lab's current stock. Some material from last year may remain. Before counting it, check its condition and whether it is still within its usable life under your storage conditions, using the product page, the certificate of analysis and your lab's records. Material that is too old for a student exercise is not stock.

Order quantity = course total − usable stock on hand

### Round to available sizes

Peptira's product pages show the sizes available. Round each order quantity up to a combination of sizes. Note the price per unit at each size when you sign in; larger sizes are often cheaper per unit, but they may not suit how the material is used if it degrades once opened.

### Check storage

Add up the storage space the order will need. Teaching labs often share storage with other courses or research groups. Make sure the space exists before the order arrives.

### Check the budget

Multiply each line by the signed-in sale price. If the total exceeds the course budget, revisit the waste factor, the number of runs, or whether every exercise needs the same material. Do not cut below what the course genuinely needs; a lab session without enough material is a worse outcome than a slightly higher order.


## Enrollment You Will Not Know Until January

The demand model depends on the number of groups, which depends on enrollment. In late November, enrollment for spring is usually an estimate. Registration may be open, waitlists may be long, and some students will drop in the first week. How should you buy?

### Build three scenarios

Run the demand model at three enrollment levels:

- **Low:** the enrollment you would see if registration ended now with typical drops.
- **Expected:** your best estimate, based on past years and current registration.
- **High:** the enrollment if sections fill to capacity and waitlists clear.

In the worked example, suppose low is 20 students per section (10 groups), expected is 24 (12 groups) and high is 28 (14 groups). The course total for the example material becomes approximately:

| Scenario | Groups/section | Course total (units) |
|---|---|---|
| Low | 10 | about 100 |
| Expected | 12 | 117 |
| High | 14 | about 135 |

(The low and high totals scale the base use and waste in proportion to the number of groups, keeping preparation and makeup fixed.)

### Buy for expected, plan for high

A common strategy is to buy enough for the expected scenario during the sale and plan a small top-up order in January if enrollment comes in high. The cost of the top-up is the difference between sale and regular price on the extra amount, which is usually modest. The cost of buying for the high scenario and ending up at low is surplus material that may age before the next offering of the course.

The arithmetic depends on the discount and on the usable life of the material. If the material stays usable for a year or more under your storage and the course runs every spring, surplus is less costly, because it can be used next year. If it does not, buying for the high scenario carries more risk.

### Use section caps

Teaching labs are usually capped by bench space and safety rules. The section cap sets an upper limit on groups per section. If sections are capped at 28 and you have four sections, the high scenario cannot exceed 56 groups. That gives you a firm ceiling for the worst case.

### Ask the registrar's office for patterns

Many registrars can tell you, from past years, how enrollment in a course changes between late November and the first week of class. That pattern is a much better guide than a guess. A five-minute email can tighten your scenarios considerably.

### Remember the credit window

Here is a detail that helps: Peptira's 10% back applies to paid orders through 11:59 PM Mountain Time on January 1, 2027. A top-up order placed in mid-December at regular price still earns 10% back. A top-up order placed after January 1 does not, unless Peptira announces a new promotion. If you can know enrollment well enough by mid-December, a top-up then captures the credit. If enrollment is not known until the first week of class, the top-up will be at regular price without the credit, but it can be partly paid with the credit earned on the sale order.

## Mapping Materials to the Semester

### Week by week

Once you have the order list, map each material to the weeks it is used. A simple calendar view helps:

| Week | Dates (illustrative) | Exercise | Materials needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Jan 19–23 | Safety orientation, techniques | Basic consumables |
| 2 | Jan 26–30 | Technique practice | Material X (small) |
| 4 | Feb 9–13 | Exercise A | Material Y, Material X |
| 9 | Mar 16–20 | Exercise B | Material Y, Material Z |
| 12 | Apr 6–10 | Exercise C | Material Z |

The dates are an invented example; use your institution's calendar.

### Why the map matters for a sale

The week map tells you when each material is needed, and therefore how long it must be stored after arriving in early December. A material used in week 2 will be stored for about seven weeks. A material used in week 12 will be stored for about four months.

For each material, compare the storage time with its usable life under your conditions. If a material used only in week 12 would be near the end of its usable life by then, consider two alternatives:

- **Buy it later.** Place a separate order closer to the week it is needed, at regular price, possibly using the credit from the sale order to offset part of the cost.
- **Buy a smaller amount now and the rest later.** Split the need between a sale order and a later order.

The discount is valuable, but not if the material degrades before students use it. A failed exercise in week 12 is a far worse outcome than a regular-price order in March.

### Handling materials once opened

For materials that degrade once opened or reconstituted, the week map also tells you how to divide them. Rather than opening one large container for the whole semester, you might plan to open one container per exercise, or prepare aliquots at the start and store them appropriately. Follow the product page and certificate of analysis for storage guidance and your lab's standard procedures for handling. Size choices in the order should reflect this plan.

## What to Buy During the Sale and What to Leave

Putting the model, the scenarios and the week map together, you can sort the course's materials into three groups.

### Buy during the sale

Materials that:

- Are needed early in the semester, or
- Stay usable well beyond the week they are needed, and
- Are needed in quantities you can predict from the expected scenario.

For these, the discount is pure benefit. Order them with `BF2026` during the weekend or `CM2026` on Monday.

### Buy partly during the sale

Materials whose total depends heavily on enrollment, or that are used across many weeks. Buy the expected-scenario amount, or the amount needed for the first part of the semester, during the sale. Plan a top-up.

### Leave for later

Materials that:

- Are used only late in the semester, and
- Have a usable life that would be strained by four months of storage, or
- Are used only in an exercise that might change after the semester starts.

Order these closer to when they are needed. The credit from the sale order can help.


## Funding a Teaching Lab Order

Teaching labs are usually funded differently from research labs, and that affects how a sale order is approved and recorded.

### Common funding sources

- **Departmental teaching budgets**, allocated per course or per program.
- **Course or lab fees** collected from students, which may be restricted to costs of the course that generated them.
- **Instructional equipment and supply grants**, from the institution or outside foundations.
- **Shared program budgets**, where several courses draw on one pool.

### Keep teaching and research purchases separate

If the same faculty member runs both a research lab and a teaching course, it can be tempting to combine orders to reach the \$250 free-shipping threshold or simply to save time. Combining is fine only if the costs are allocated correctly to each fund afterward. Course fee revenue, in particular, is often restricted to the course that collected it, and using it for research supplies can be a problem. When in doubt, place separate orders, each with its own code. Under Peptira's terms each order can carry `BF2026`, `CM2026`, `PEPTIDESAVE10` or `10OFF`, all worth the same.

### Fiscal year and spending deadlines

Many U.S. universities use a July–June fiscal year, so a November sale is mid-year and does not usually face year-end pressure. Some departmental teaching budgets, however, are allocated per semester or per academic year with their own deadlines. Check whether spring materials should be charged to the fall or spring budget, and whether a November order for spring use is acceptable to your department.

### Approvals

Departments often require approval from the department chair, program director or business manager for purchases above a certain amount. Get approval before the sale, using the demand model and order list as the justification. A clear model makes approval quick: it shows exactly how the quantity was derived.

### Payment

Teaching labs typically order with a departmental purchasing card. Check the card's transaction and monthly limits before the sale. If the order is large, ask for a temporary increase in advance rather than splitting the order to fit the limit, which card policies often prohibit.

## Safety Documentation and Training

Teaching labs bring together many inexperienced people in one room, which is why they are usually covered by detailed safety requirements. The sale does not change any of them, but a new material or a larger quantity may trigger some.

### Check that the material is approved for the course

Your institution's chemical hygiene plan, required for laboratories under the OSHA Laboratory Standard at 29 CFR 1910.1450, may require that materials used in teaching labs be reviewed or approved. If you are adding a new material to an exercise, check before ordering.

### Have the safety information ready

Make sure safety data sheets and any other required safety information for each material are available in the teaching lab before students use it, as your institution's program requires. If Peptira provides documentation for a product, download it when you order.

### Review handling procedures

For each exercise, the lab manual or standard operating procedure should describe how students handle the material, what personal protective equipment they use, and how waste is disposed of. The National Academies' *Prudent Practices in the Laboratory* is a widely used reference for building these procedures. If the materials involve biological components, your institution's biosafety program and guidance such as the CDC and NIH's *Biosafety in Microbiological and Biomedical Laboratories* apply.

### Train the TAs

Teaching assistants who prepare and supervise exercises need to know how to store, prepare and handle each material. Brief them on any new materials before the semester starts.

### Students and the purchaser age requirement

Peptira sells only to buyers 21 or older, and its products are for in vitro research. In a teaching course, the institution or instructor is the purchaser, and students work with the materials only within supervised laboratory exercises under the institution's safety program. Materials should never leave the teaching lab.

## Handing Off to Next Year

Teaching labs change hands often. A coordinator moves on, a TA graduates, a new instructor takes over the course. The demand model and the records from this sale are the most valuable things to pass along.

### What to leave for the next coordinator

- **The demand model spreadsheet**, with sections, groups, runs, amounts, waste factors, preparation and makeup for each exercise.
- **Actual usage** recorded at the end of the semester, so the waste factor can be updated.
- **The order list and confirmations** from this sale.
- **The week map**, showing when each material is used.
- **Storage notes**, including where materials are kept and any condition checks.
- **Supplier account details**, held in a way the department can access, not in a personal email.
- **Lessons learned**, a few lines on what went well and what did not.

### Recording actual usage

At the end of each exercise, ask the TAs to record how much material was actually used, including waste. At the end of the semester, compare actual use with the model. The difference tells you whether the waste factor, preparation allowance or amount per run needs adjusting. After two or three semesters, the model becomes very accurate.

### A one-page course supply summary

Some departments keep a one-page summary per course: materials, annual quantity, supplier, typical cost, storage, and notes. It sits in the department's shared drive and is updated each year. When the next sale comes, the coordinator can start from it instead of from scratch.

## Mistakes That Are Common in Teaching-Lab Buying

### Buying for capacity, not enrollment

Ordering for every seat in every section is easy and safe, but it often produces large surpluses. Use the expected scenario and a top-up plan.

### Forgetting preparation

TAs practicing a new exercise can use a surprising amount of material. Include preparation in the model.

### Ignoring the week map

Buying everything in November for a course that ends in May risks materials degrading before late exercises. Map materials to weeks and compare with usable life.

### Mixing teaching and research stock

Shared storage makes it easy for teaching materials to be used by a research group over the break, or vice versa. Label and track by course.

### Leaving receiving to chance

A package that arrives during finals week with no one assigned to receive it can sit unopened past the seven-day claims window. Assign a person.

### Assuming the order can be adjusted

Peptira's sales are final and orders cannot be canceled or modified. If enrollment drops sharply after ordering, the surplus stays. That is another reason to buy for expected enrollment rather than capacity.


## Coordinating Across a Department

Many departments run several laboratory courses each spring, often with overlapping materials. A little coordination can lower costs and reduce waste, as long as each course's budget and records stay clear.

### A shared materials list

Ask each course coordinator to submit their order list, with quantities and the weeks of use, to one person by a set date before the sale. Combine the lists into a department view showing which materials appear in more than one course.

### Where coordination helps

- **Larger sizes.** If two courses use the same material, combining their needs may justify a larger size with a lower unit price, provided the material can be divided and stored so each course gets its share in good condition.
- **Free shipping.** Small courses whose orders fall below \$250 can combine with others to reach the threshold, if costs are allocated afterward.
- **Shared buffer.** Rather than each course keeping its own safety margin, the department can hold a modest shared buffer for materials used in several courses. That reduces the total surplus.
- **One receiving plan.** A single person receives and checks the department's sale orders, records the unboxing videos, and distributes materials to each course's labeled storage.

### Where coordination hurts

- **Unclear allocation.** If no one records which course paid for what, budget questions follow. Keep a simple allocation table.
- **Different timing.** If one course needs a material in week 2 and another in week 14, a combined purchase means the second course's share sits for months. The week map should guide whether combining makes sense.
- **Single point of failure.** If one person handles all orders and they are away during the sale, nothing gets ordered. Name a backup.

### A department-level summary

After the sale, the coordinator of the combined order can produce a one-page summary for the department: total spent, total checkout savings, credit earned, and how each course's share was allocated. That makes the next year's planning faster and shows the department the value of coordinated buying.

## Stretching a Teaching Budget Without Cutting Corners

Teaching budgets are often tight. The sale helps, but there are other ways to make the money go further without compromising what students learn or how safely they learn it.

### Revisit amounts per run

Exercises are sometimes designed with generous quantities that were never revisited. If an exercise works reliably with less material per run, a smaller amount per run, validated by the instructor or TA, reduces cost every year. Do this before the semester, not by improvising during a lab session.

### Reduce avoidable waste

Waste in teaching labs is partly unavoidable, but some of it comes from predictable causes: unclear instructions, unfamiliar equipment, materials left out too long. Better pre-lab instructions, a short demonstration and clear labeling of aliquots can lower the waste factor. Record the result; next year's order shrinks accordingly.

### Prepare shared stocks carefully

Preparing shared stock solutions or aliquots centrally, rather than having each group prepare its own from the original container, can reduce waste and improve consistency, if done according to the lab's procedures and the product's storage guidance.

### Use the credit deliberately

The 10% back from the sale order is a real resource for the course. Applying it to the January top-up or to next year's order lowers the effective cost of the course's materials. Record it in the course budget so it is not forgotten.

### Do not stretch safety

Cutting personal protective equipment, safety training or appropriate waste disposal to save money is never acceptable. The savings from the sale should make it easier, not harder, to run the course properly.
